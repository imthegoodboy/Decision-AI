AI assistants increasingly help people choose phones, insurers and courses. They cite web sources, which makes their advice look checkable. We test Track 2's question directly: when an AI agent uses a reliability and provenance tool, does its behaviour improve? We built Decision AI, which binds each claim to a verbatim quote, checks it against its source and all other sources under fixed rules, scores reliability from page metadata and from verification, and seals the evidence in a hash-chained audit log. We exposed this layer as a tool that agents call. Claude agents answered {{raw:n_questions}} real decision questions from identical live-web sources, with or without the tool, before and after we planted one credible-looking article containing three false claims ({{raw:n_agent_runs}} agent runs, blinded judges, {{int:judging.claims_judged}} judged claims). Citation faithfulness was already near ceiling (about 98%) in every condition. No tool-using answer stated a planted falsehood as fact, against {{pct0:poisoned.planted_adopted_any.baseline}} of baseline answers. Yet all conditions flagged the planted claims and still followed them. In {{rawpct:stability.pooled.attack_consistent_poisoned}} of cases the recommendation moved the way the planted article pushed, against {{rawpct:stability.pooled.attack_consistent_replicate}} between two clean runs ({{pv:stability.pooled.mcnemar_attack_p}}). The tool reduced this steering but did not remove it. Metadata-based reliability ranked the fake article above most genuine sources; reliability earned through cross-source verification halved its advantage. Detecting misinformation is not the same as being unaffected by it: trust infrastructure must constrain decisions, not only claims.
